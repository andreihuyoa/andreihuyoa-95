I have had the same thought more times than I can count: **I know I saved it somewhere.**

Maybe it was a restaurant I wanted to try, a design reference I wanted to return to, or a tutorial that felt too useful to lose. I remember the idea clearly enough to know that I cared about it. What I cannot remember is whether I saved it on Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, or somewhere in my browser.

So I start looking.

I open one app, scroll through a collection, and try to recognize a thumbnail. Then I move to another app and do the same thing again. If I eventually find the post, I may still need to watch the whole video again just to recover the one detail that mattered.

The link was saved. The reason I saved it was not.

## Saving feels like taking care of something

Two instagram reels from [Kathryn](https://www.instagram.com/kathryn.tatess/), put words to a pattern I had already felt but had never properly identified.

In [the first reel](https://www.instagram.com/p/DdkL_kUxbBy/), she explains that the internet is excellent at helping us discover new things, but much worse at helping us return to them. In [the second](https://www.instagram.com/p/DbUDo6kx78U/), she makes another important distinction: when we keep saving content but struggle to use it later, what looks like a motivation problem _(topic for another day)_ may actually be a systems problem.

That distinction matters because pressing Save feels like progress.

For a moment, the small pressure to remember disappears. I do not need to read the article now, write down the restaurant, or try the idea today. I have placed it somewhere safe for a future version of myself.

But “saved” quietly becomes “handled,” even though we haven't really done an action yet. The button closes the moment without creating a real plan for what comes next.

Saving is useful. It lets us keep moving instead of stopping for everything interesting that crosses our screens. The problem begins when an action designed for temporary relief becomes the entire system.

## A saved post loses its why

A platform usually remembers the content and the time it was saved. It does not necessarily remember the context around it.

It does not know that I saved a restaurant because I wanted somewhere to go with a friend next Saturday. It does not know that a long video contained one technique I wanted to test in my own work. It does not know whether I wanted to finish something, reference it during a project, or simply keep it because it made me feel something.

Those intentions can look identical inside a saved folder.

Some saves are keepsakes. They do not need to lead anywhere. Some are references that only need to be findable when a related problem appears. Others are promises to ourselves: _I want to do something with this later._

The third kind is where “later” starts to break down. Later is not a time, a place, or a plan. It is an optimistic label we attach to something before returning to the feed.

When the original context fades, future me inherits a link but not the decision that gave that saved link meaning.

## The effort begins when we try to return

When I am finally ready to act, the system asks me to reconstruct the entire journey.

First I have to remember that I saved something. Then I have to remember where I saved it. I have to find it among everything I saved afterward, open it, and consume enough of it again to understand why it once felt important.

Each step is small. Together, they create enough friction to make searching again—or giving up—feel easier.

This is not actually a new problem. In 2001, a [Microsoft Research study](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/keeping-found-things-found-web/) observed how people kept web information for later use. Some people emailed links to themselves because an email did more than store a URL, it returned to an inbox they already checked, could include a note about why the link mattered, and could help them remember what needed to happen next.

Traditional bookmarks, the researchers noted, often provided neither that reminding function nor that context of relevance.

The internet has changed enormously since then. Our saved information is no longer limited to web pages and browser bookmarks. It now includes short videos, carousels, threads, screenshots, products, places, and conversations spread across platforms.

But the central problem still feels familiar. We have become very good at putting things away. We have not become equally good at bringing them back.

## A place we can actually return to

I do not think the answer is to turn every interesting post into a task. That would replace one kind of overload with another.

I also do not think we need to carefully categorize every link the moment we find it. A system that demands too much effort at the point of saving will be abandoned, especially when saving happens in the middle of something else.

What I want is simpler: a dependable place for the things I chose to keep, and a way for the important ones to return at a useful moment.

That means retrieval cannot be treated as an afterthought. A saved item should be easy to find even when I remember the idea but not the platform, creator, title, or date. When a save carries an intention, I should be able to preserve that intention while it is still fresh. If it matters later, I should not have to rely on chance to see it again.

The goal is not to remember everything. It is to reduce the distance between remembering that something exists and being able to use it.

## Where Fig enters the story

This question eventually became part of why I started building Fig.

Fig is an attempt to give saved links from social media and the web one home—somewhere they can be organized, searched, and returned to without remembering which platform originally held them. For the saves that represent something I genuinely want to revisit, reminders can create a path back instead of leaving that intention buried in a folder.

I am not building it because I think another app can manufacture motivation. A notification cannot make a person read, cook, visit, learn, or create. It can only return an intention to their attention and make the next step easier to see.

That is a smaller promise, but I think it is a more honest one.

## What should “saved” mean?

I used to think saving was the final action: I found something valuable, pressed a button, and kept it.

Now I think saving is only the beginning of a relationship with that information. Sometimes the relationship ends immediately, and that is fine. Not everything deserves to become knowledge or action. But when something does matter, the system should help it survive beyond the moment of discovery.

“Saved” should mean more than _this still exists somewhere_. It should mean _I can trust myself to find this again when it becomes useful._

The internet gave us a Save button. What many of us are still missing is a way back.
